Conflicts

Stories live from characters, and characters are explored best in conflicts.
A Conflict describes the confrontation between several characters on different levels, in which the respective current goals of the characters partially or entirely exclude one another.

Concretely, this means that a Conflict does not only consist of two characters standing in each other's way, with the situation only solvable through a fight to the death, but can depict the most varied situations. A few examples of conflicts of different natures are listed here:

  • At a market stall, an elderly lady tries to push the price of an item as low as possible through haggling, while the seller wants to lower the price as little as necessary. Both characters pursue opposing goals, and from this a Conflict arises that is won with rhetoric.
  • In a narrow alley, a young lad flees from a gang of thugs. The boy tries to shake off his pursuers, while they must not allow this. This Conflict is a chase through the alley, in which skill is required.
  • The murderer of a civilian has been arrested and is now sitting in an interrogation. The perpetrator is guilty, but has prepared very strong answers for every question. The interrogator must now determine from the gaps in the answers that there is a problem with the alibi. This Conflict is based on the intellect of the characters.
  • In a quiz show, the two finalists are in the sudden-death round - whoever can answer the question first wins. Here, both parties are working against each other again, but this time no attribute itself is called for, but the clever application of character knowledge.

Defining a Conflict

A Conflict is defined by the following properties:

  • A series of Factions, each with at least one goal of their own.
    A Faction in a Conflict consists of a group of characters who pursue the same goals in this Conflict. In the vast majority of scenarios, all player characters are part of the same Faction. For all other characters, the Director can prepare a pool of Factions and spontaneously assign unexpectedly appearing characters to one of the prepared Factions, or put these characters into their own Factions.
  • Every goal needs a specific Win Condition and an optional Lose Condition. These two conditions must exclude one another. As soon as one of the conditions occurs, this goal counts as completed. When a Faction has completed all goals, it usually subsequently tries to end the Conflict as quickly as possible.

Playing Out a Conflict

Conflicts are played out in two ways: either it is a simple Conflict, in which both parties act without time-critical actions, or the Conflict is carried out with a turn order.

Simple Conflicts

In a simple Conflict, every character can perform actions. This happens as in any other Scene, by the characters declaring their actions to the Director, and the Director then either granting the action or requiring a Check.

An existing Conflict counts as ended as soon as the Win or Lose Conditions of all goals of all involved Factions have occurred.

As soon as an action is time-critical, i.e. the execution of an action has consequences in which the sequence matters, the Conflict focuses on that acting character.

Focused Conflicts

While the Focus is on individual characters, the respective character stands at the center of the Scene. Instead of every character in the Scene being able to perform actions freely, only one character with Focus can act. The character in Focus performs an action, after which the Focus passes to another character.

If a character achieves a Critical Success on a Check while in Focus, that character's Actor may tell the story themselves for the duration of that action.

The First Focus

As soon as a character performs a time-critical action, the events focus on that action. Who performs this action is decided by the characters within the story themselves, by performing this action first.

Actions in Focus

After an action, the Focus switches to a character outside the Faction who is affected by the action just performed. If no obvious characters present themselves, for example because the character helped another character within the Faction, the Director decides.

Instead of the Focus falling to another Faction, the character in Focus can spend 1 Adrenaline to keep the Focus with their own Faction and instead select a character from their own Faction who is next in Focus.

Attacks

Attacks against other characters are special actions. They aim to deliberately inflict Injuries on the target character.

An Attack can be performed unarmed or with a Tool. An Attack is always a Check based on the type of attack - fast, precise attacks are based on Grace, while powerful attacks are usually performed with Might. Attacks with complex systems usually use Wits, and Charms is used for verbal attacks.

The Check is performed as a Mixed Check with the Tool, which adds a Tool die to the Check via its Quality. Unarmed attacks are equipped with Quality 1 by default, which mixes a D4 into the Check. Further details on using Tools can be found in the corresponding chapter.

Like any Check, attacks are also influenced by facilitating and complicating circumstances, and correspondingly given an Advantage or Disadvantage. It is also a Disadvantage when a character tries to perform an attack deliberately targeted, i.e. to hit a weak point of the target, or to bypass worn armor.

The Severity of the resulting Harm can be recognized from the outcome of the Check:

  • a Success produces Harm of Severity 1
  • a Critical Success produces Harm of Severity 2
  • a Partial Success can produce Harm of Severity 1, but in return produces a complication in the story for the character

How to deal with the produced Harm is described in the chapter 05. Vitalität.

The End of Focus

A Conflict is no longer in Focus as soon as no character performs actions that have a time-critical sequence. From that point on, events continue as a normal Scene, and every character can perform any number of actions as usual.
